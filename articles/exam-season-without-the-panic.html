Helping Your Child Through Exam Season, Without the Panic

VN
Vikram Nair Educator & Exam Coach, M.Ed

Updated 24 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer: The single biggest thing that reduces exam stress at home is separating your child’s worth from their result, said out loud and repeatedly, before results ever arrive. Beyond that: protect sleep over extra revision hours, keep one non-negotiable break in the day, and ask about their day before you ask about their score.

Why exam season feels different this year

Board exams carry weight that ordinary tests don’t — they feel like a verdict rather than a checkpoint, both to the child sitting them and often to the household around them. That pressure is real and doesn’t need to be talked away. What can change is how much of it lands on your child versus how much of it you can absorb as the parent.

What actually helps at home during exams

Protect sleep over extra revision hours

An extra hour of sleep consistently outperforms an extra hour of tired revision for memory and focus the next day. If a choice has to be made between the two in the final weeks, sleep wins.

Keep one non-negotiable break, every day

A short walk, a favourite show, time with friends — something that has nothing to do with the exam. Removing every break to make room for “just a bit more studying” usually reduces retention, not improves it.

Ask about their day before you ask about their score

Leading every conversation with “how did it go” teaches a child that the exam is the only thing you’re interested in. Ask how they’re feeling first.

Keep the household’s mood steady

Children pick up on a parent’s anxiety about their exams faster than on anything said directly. A calm household, even a quietly nervous one, is worth more than any pep talk.

What to avoid saying, even with good intentions

  • “Your whole future depends on this.” It raises the stakes without raising performance, and it isn’t even true.
  • Comparing to a sibling, cousin or classmate’s marks. It adds pressure without adding useful information.
  • “I’m not worried, you’ll be fine” said dismissively. If your child is worried, this can feel like their feelings weren’t heard, rather than reassuring.

If your child is showing signs of real anxiety

A little pre-exam nervousness is normal and even useful. It is worth a closer look, and a conversation with a school counsellor or your paediatrician, if you notice a sustained drop in sleep or appetite, withdrawal from friends, panic symptoms before studying, or your child speaking about their own worth only in terms of marks.

After the results, whatever they are

The conversation that happens after results matters as much as anything said before them. Lead with how your child is feeling, not with the number, and keep the door open for what comes next rather than closing it with judgment either way.

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to what parents ask us most about exam season.

Should I reduce my child’s activities during exam season?

Cutting extracurriculars entirely often backfires, because they are frequently what keeps stress regulated. A short, protected break most days does more for exam performance than a total study lockdown.

Is it normal for my child to lose sleep before exams?

Occasional pre-exam nerves affecting sleep are common. Sustained sleep loss over the whole exam season is not something to normalise, and is one of the clearest early signs that stress needs active management, since sleep loss itself worsens both mood and recall.

How do I talk to my child about a bad practice test result?

Lead with curiosity about what happened before you lead with the number: which section was hardest, was it a knowledge gap or a timing problem. A practice test is diagnostic information, not a preview of the real result.

What are signs my child’s exam stress has become a bigger problem?

Watch for a persistent drop in sleep or appetite, withdrawal from friends and family, panic symptoms before study or exams, or your child talking about their own worth only in terms of marks. These warrant a conversation with a counsellor or your paediatrician, not just reassurance at home.
