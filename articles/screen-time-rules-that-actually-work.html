Screen Time Rules That Actually Work: An Age-by-Age Guide

By the ParentCircle Editorial Team · Reviewed by Paediatricians · Updated 24 September 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer: Most guidance suggests no recreational screens before 18 months, about 1 hour a day for a 2–5 year old, roughly 1–2 hours on a school day for a 6–12 year old, and a family-agreed limit rather than a fixed number for teenagers. The number matters less than picking one, agreeing it with your child in advance, and holding it consistently every day.

How much screen time is actually okay, by age

Every age band has slightly different guidance, and the right number for your family also depends on what the screen time is for — a video call with grandparents is not the same as an hour of short-form video.

AgeTypical recreational screen time
Under 18 monthsAvoid, except video calls with family
18 months – 2 yearsMinimal, always watched together
2–5 yearsAround 1 hour a day, high-quality content
6–12 yearsRoughly 1–2 hours on a school day
13–18 yearsA family-agreed limit, reviewed together

Treat this table as a starting point for a conversation, not a rule to enforce silently. Children stick to limits they had a hand in setting far more than limits that were simply announced.

Why screen-time fights happen

Most screen time arguments aren’t really about the screen. They’re about the transition — being pulled out of something engaging with no warning, by a rule that feels like it appeared from nowhere. That is why the same limit, introduced two different ways, produces two very different evenings.

Five screen time rules that actually work

  1. Agree the rule when everyone is calm — not in the middle of a session, when the ask is guaranteed to feel unfair.
  2. Use a visible timer so the countdown belongs to the clock, not to you. It turns off far fewer arguments than a parent saying “time’s up.”
  3. Give a two-minute warning before the screen goes off, so your child can finish the level or the scene they’re on.
  4. Keep the rule the same on weekends and holidays, or state the exception in advance. A limit that moves without warning stops feeling like a limit at all.
  5. Model it yourself. A rule that only applies to the child is the one that gets tested hardest.

What to do when your child pushes back

Expect testing, especially in the first two weeks of a new rule — that is normal, not a sign the rule is wrong. Hold the limit calmly and identically each time, and save the conversation about whether the rule itself is fair for a separate, calmer moment, not the moment the screen goes off.

When to worry about screen time

The number of hours matters less than what screen time is replacing. It is worth a closer look, and a conversation with your paediatrician, if screens are consistently displacing sleep, outdoor play, in-person friendships or homework, or if your child becomes unusually distressed whenever a screen is removed.

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to what parents ask us most about screen time.

How much screen time should a 7-year-old have on a school day?

Most paediatric guidance suggests around 1 hour of recreational screen time on a school day for a primary-school-aged child, on top of any screen time used for schoolwork. The exact limit matters less than having one that is consistent and enforced daily.

Should toddlers under 2 have any screen time?

General guidance recommends avoiding screens for children under 18 months except for video calls with family, and keeping screen time minimal and always co-viewed with a parent up to age 2.

What is the best way to enforce a screen time limit without a fight?

Agree the rule with your child in advance, when everyone is calm, rather than announcing it in the moment. Use a visible timer so the countdown is not coming only from you, and give a two-minute warning before the screen goes off.

Does screen time before bed affect sleep?

Yes. Screens close to bedtime are linked to delayed sleep onset in children, partly from the content itself and partly from blue light suppressing melatonin. A screen-free window of 30 to 60 minutes before bed is a widely recommended rule.
