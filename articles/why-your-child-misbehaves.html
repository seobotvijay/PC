Why Your Child Misbehaves, and What Actually Helps

AR
Anjali Rao Certified Positive Parenting Coach

Updated 24 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer: Most misbehaviour is communication, not manipulation — a child telling you they’re tired, overwhelmed, hungry, or don’t yet have the skill to do what’s being asked. Behaviour changes fastest when you name the unmet need first and teach the missing skill, rather than punishing the behaviour on its own.

Behaviour is communication, not defiance

Before a child can explain what’s wrong in words, the feeling has to come out somewhere — and it usually comes out as behaviour. A refusal, a meltdown, a slammed door: these are rarely a calculated attempt to make your day harder. They’re closer to a check-engine light than an act of rebellion.

That reframe matters because it changes the question you ask. Instead of “how do I stop this behaviour,” the more useful question is “what is this behaviour telling me?”

The four most common reasons behind “bad” behaviour

  • Tired, hungry or overstimulated. The single biggest cause of meltdowns in young children, and the easiest to miss because it feels too simple.
  • An unmet need for attention. If the fastest way to get noticed is to misbehave, children will reliably choose it — negative attention beats no attention.
  • A skill gap. Sharing, waiting, losing gracefully — these are skills, not instincts. A child who hasn’t been taught one yet will look like they’re refusing to use it.
  • Testing a limit. Especially after a rule changes, children test to see if it’s real. This is developmentally normal, not a sign something has gone wrong.

What actually helps, situation by situation

The public meltdown

Step aside from the audience rather than trying to reason in the moment. Most of what makes a public meltdown feel unbearable is the watching, not the child.

The daily power struggle

Offer a real, limited choice instead of an order. “Shoes on now or in two minutes” gives back a sliver of control without giving up the outcome.

The repeated rule-breaking

Check whether the rule was ever actually explained, or just assumed. A rule stated once, weeks ago, is not a rule a young child reliably remembers under pressure.

Consequences that teach vs. consequences that just punish

A consequence teaches when it’s connected to what happened and delivered calmly: a toy that was thrown gets put away for the rest of the day. A consequence just punishes when it’s unrelated and delivered in anger: no screen time for a week because of a mealtime meltdown. The first builds understanding. The second mostly builds resentment.

When to get extra support

Most behaviour challenges are a normal, if exhausting, part of raising a child. It’s worth talking to a child psychologist or paediatrician if a behaviour is intense, happening daily, lasting more than a few weeks, showing up across both home and school, or if your child seems distressed by their own behaviour and unable to stop it.

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to what parents ask us most about difficult behaviour.

Is my child manipulating me when they act out?

True manipulation requires a level of planning most children, especially under 8, don’t yet have. What looks like manipulation is usually a child who has learned, through repetition, which behaviour reliably gets a need met — which is a habit to redirect, not a character trait to punish.

Do time-outs work?

Time-outs can interrupt a spiralling moment, but they teach more when paired with a conversation afterwards about what happened and what to do differently next time. Used alone, repeatedly, they tend to lose effectiveness.

How do I stay calm when my child is having a meltdown?

Lower your voice instead of raising it, and give yourself a scripted first line to say, such as “I’m right here.” A meltdown almost always ends faster when it isn’t met with a second one from the parent.

When does difficult behaviour need professional help?

Consider speaking to a paediatrician or child psychologist if the behaviour is intense, frequent, lasting more than a few weeks, appearing across multiple settings such as home and school, or if your child seems distressed by their own behaviour and unable to stop it.
